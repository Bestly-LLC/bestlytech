# Plain-language brand copy

## The rule

**Marketing is for people who don't understand what you do. That's why they hire you.**

Write for the person who has never heard of the product, has no interest in the category, and is giving you three seconds. Say what it is, then what they get. The technical detail is *proof you offer later*, never the opening pitch.

## Four tests every line must pass

1. **Would a stranger know what this product IS after reading it?** If the first thing they read is an ingredient name or a packaging format, rewrite it.
2. **Does it say what THEY get, or what WE did?** Manufacturing process is not a benefit. Translate it.
3. **Is there a word they'd have to look up?** Cut it or explain it in the same breath.
4. **Could you say it out loud to a stranger without feeling like a brochure?** If not, it's written to sound smart. Smart is the enemy here.

## Never open with

- A chemical or ingredient name (`hypochlorous acid`, `HOCl`)
- A packaging or manufacturing term (`bag-on-valve`, `airless`, `vacuum-sealed`, `electrolysis`)
- Chemical formulas in body copy (`H₂O`, `NaCl`)
- Industry shorthand (`actives`, `barrier`, `INCI`, `ppm`, `routine creep`, `the 1% line`)
- Cleverness that costs clarity ("Weighable, verifiable, boring", "Mild enough that age is not the deciding factor")

These words can appear *later*, as the proof — never as the hook.

## Translate mechanism into benefit

| Don't say | Say |
|---|---|
| Bag-on-valve, airless, vacuum-sealed | It comes in a sealed can. Air never gets back in. |
| No air is drawn back in | The last spray works like the first one did. |
| Electrolysis converts H₂O and NaCl | Run an electric current through salt water. |
| Hypochlorous acid | The same thing your body makes to keep itself clean. |
| Free of alcohol, fragrance, and oils | No alcohol, no fragrance, no oils — the three things most likely to set skin off. |
| Non-comedogenic / won't clog pores | (check whether the claim is even substantiated first) |

## Structure

- **Short sentences. One idea each.** Target roughly 12 words per sentence.
- **Aim for a 6th–8th grade reading level.** Verify it — don't assume.
- Headline says what it is. Subhead says what you do with it. Detail comes third.
- Prefer concrete over abstract: "spray it on, let it dry" beats "a refreshing daily ritual."

## Verify, don't assume

After writing, run a jargon sweep over the *rendered* text — including SVG diagrams, image alt text, meta descriptions, marquees, button labels, and the eyebrow above the headline. Those hide from a normal read and are often where the jargon survives. Compute the reading grade before and after; report both.

## HOKU claim constraints (these override style)

Never write, in any channel:

- "first" or "only of its kind" — unsubstantiated superiority claim (FTC requires prior substantiation; NAD does not treat #1 claims as puffery)
- "dermatologist tested", "non-cytotoxic" — not backed by a study we can point to
- kill / disinfect / sanitize / treat / heal / antimicrobial — these make it a **drug** under 21 CFR 201.128, not a cosmetic
- "100x more powerful than bleach" — misrepresents the underlying source
- "Made in USA" or any origin claim — unresolved until component sourcing is confirmed (FTC 16 CFR 323 "all or virtually all"; California Bus. & Prof. Code § 17533.7 is stricter)
- Marketing HOCl for **hard surfaces / killing germs on objects** — that's a pesticide claim under FIFRA requiring EPA registration. Face only.

Safe ground: what's in it, what isn't in it, how the can works, how to use it, and what the molecule is in factual biological terms.
